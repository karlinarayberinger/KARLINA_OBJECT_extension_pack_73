Each might sincerely say, “I am the original.” Yet they would be two physical implementations and therefore two numerically distinct conscious events. As soon as they received different inputs, their states and subjective histories would diverge.

This is the distinction between:

* **Qualitative identity:** (A) and (B) initially have exactly the same properties or informational organization.

* **Numerical identity:** (A) and (B) are literally one and the same particular entity.

Perfect replication could plausibly preserve the first without preserving the second.

The original consciousness would not become a single observer looking simultaneously through both replicas. Instead, there would apparently be two streams, each possessing an identical remembered past but acquiring a different experienced future. Their memories of being the original would establish psychological continuity with the original, not exclusive numerical identity with it.

A useful term is **forking**. Until replication, there is one causal history. After replication, there are multiple successor histories:

[preformatted_text]

H_original = {
                H_A
                H_B
             }

[end preformatted_text]

Neither successor needs to be considered fraudulent. Both may authentically inherit the original pattern. But what cannot survive the fork is the claim that one numerically singular instance of consciousness remains singular while occupying both branches.

There is also an epistemic problem. Imagine that the original hardware is destroyed immediately after creating one perfect replica. The replica awakens with uninterrupted memories and reports that the transfer succeeded. That report would be expected under at least two hypotheses:

1. The original stream of consciousness continued in the new hardware.

2. The original stream ended, and a new conscious instance began with memories of continuity.

From the outside—and possibly from the replica’s own perspective—those hypotheses might be empirically indistinguishable. The replica’s certainty that it “continued” would not prove that the original subject’s first-person awareness migrated. A newly created duplicate would possess exactly the memories needed to feel continuous.

Duplication makes the problem clearer. If two replicas are produced, both cannot be the one numerically unique continuation of the original in the ordinary one-to-one meaning of identity. This suggests that the copying procedure preserves structure, memory, personality, and causal ancestry more readily than it preserves a unique subjective token.

I would therefore phrase your conclusion this way:

> A conscious informational pattern might be copied exactly, and each adequate implementation might generate an experientially indistinguishable successor consciousness. Nevertheless, each implementation would constitute a distinct occurrence of consciousness rather than a reappearance of the original occurrence.

One small terminological refinement: I would say that the original informational pattern was **instantiated by** the original hardware, rather than that consciousness was simply derived from hardware. Conscious experience may depend not merely on a static stored pattern but on its ongoing causal execution—state transitions, timing, feedback, embodiment, and interaction with an environment. A verbatim copy would have to reproduce every functionally relevant part of that dynamic organization, not merely a snapshot of memories or recorded behavior.

Applied to KARBYTES, an archive could enable arbitrarily many karbytes-like successors. They might remember creating KARBYTES, identify with its author, recognize The Burrow and the BGT, and continue the project coherently. But informational fidelity would not demonstrate that the presently experiencing karbytes had awakened inside them. They would be new subjective centers inheriting karbytes’ informational ancestry.

So replication may reproduce the **kind and contents** of a consciousness without reproducing the **same occurrence** of consciousness. It preserves a pattern across implementations, but it does not obviously transport the original first-person point of view from one implementation to another.
