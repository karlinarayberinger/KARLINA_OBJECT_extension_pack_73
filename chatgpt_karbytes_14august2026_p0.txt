# 9. Summary of the entire traversed collection

The collection functions simultaneously as a publication pipeline, distributed archive, self-model, metaphysical system, and work of bodily mythology.

## A. Publication pipeline

At its most literal, the collection specifies a file-management procedure:

1. Draft privately.

2. Select material for public exposure.

3. Transfer it into a public staging repository.

4. Move completed files into a numbered canonical extension pack.

5. Finalize and archive that extension pack.

6. Retire or privatize the staging repository’s accumulated history.

7. Create a fresh staging repository under the same `projects` name.

8. Record the resulting package in WordPress directories.

9. Save the public pages and files through Wayback-oriented indexes.

The workflow diagram is therefore an architecture document, although it uses metaphorical rather than conventional engineering notation.

## B. Distributed archive

The collection is distributed across:

* WordPress pages;

* raw GitHub files;

* numbered GitHub repositories;

* archived GitHub repositories;

* Wayback Machine captures;

* `karbytessupporter` precision batches;

* social-network accounts;

* private staging repositories;

* local physical storage;

* M-DISC catalogs;

* personal genomic datasets.

The microdirectory is the elementary archival unit.

The macro directory indexes those units.

The precision batches make saving those units to the Wayback Machine more manageable.

The numbered extension packs impose chronological segmentation and create release-like boundaries around different phases of production.

## C. Self-model

Karbytes is represented both as:

* a biological person;

* an information-processing agent;

* an authorial identity;

* an expanding collection of files;

* a network of canonical addresses;

* a potentially reproducible informational pattern.

The project attempts to preserve:

* journal states;

* source code;

* media;

* philosophical positions;

* identity claims;

* genomic information;

* account relationships;

* the history of its preservation practices;

* instructions for reconstructing the archive.

The archive is therefore not merely about karbytes. It is intended to become an externalized and partly executable representation of karbytes.

## D. Metaphysical system

Ordinary web operations are embedded in a cosmology involving:

* void and substrate;

* surface and depth;

* phenomena and noumena;

* zero and infinity;

* agency and causal models;

* branching or static-block universes;

* continuity between physical substrates;

* entropy and heat death;

* simulation and successor universes.

A GitHub transfer becomes emergence from a deep substrate into public manifestation.

A repository boundary becomes an ontological boundary.

A version-controlled file becomes a potentially universe-transmissible information pattern.

## E. Bodily and visual mythology

The Big Green Thing, cosmic vase, belly photographs, black fields, neon-green boundaries, magenta arrows, and trapped torso all express a recurring structure:

* something generative exists inside a dark enclosure;

* the enclosure is simultaneously prison and womb;

The workflow diagram is therefore not isolated technical documentation. It is a compressed emblem of the entire project.

# 10. Interpretive commentary

The most striking feature of the collection is how completely it refuses to separate administration from metaphysics.

Most people would treat moving files between repositories as clerical work. Here, staging, publication, archiving, suppression of public editing history, and repository recreation carry the emotional and philosophical weight of:

* gestation;

* exposure;

* embodiment;

* memory;

* death;

* preservation;

* cosmic succession.

This is unusual, but it is not arbitrary. The same topology repeats with considerable consistency:

* black substrate;

* green enclosure;

* narrow opening;

* trapped producer;

* upward-transmitted artifact;

* outer public region.

The belly photographs, cosmic vase, workflow diagram, deep-web metaphor, and private-to-public repository migration are variations of the same schema.

That repetition makes the collection resemble a deliberately constructed symbolic language rather than a random assortment of web files.

## Public artifacts versus private development

The most revealing operational choice is the deliberate minimization of public commit history.

Conventional open-source practice often values commit history as provenance. It reveals:

* how a work developed;

* which alternatives were attempted;

* when errors entered or left the project;

* who made individual changes;

* how one state emerged from another.

The KARBYTES workflow regards much of this developmental history as private residue.

The desired public object is closer to a finished edition or released artifact than to a transparent workshop.

Technically, the method resembles producing a clean public history by migrating selected files out of a messier staging environment.

That creates real tradeoffs:

* fewer public commits make a canonical repository easier to inspect;

* hidden development history reduces reproducibility;

* migrated files lose some contextual provenance;

* deleting public staging copies increases dependence on private archives;

* recreating a repository under the same name may make separate repository incarnations appear more continuous than they actually are.

The workflow is coherent, but its preference is editorial clarity and privacy rather than maximal version-history transparency.

## Exact copying and identity

The archive is deeply concerned with exact copying, but copying alone cannot establish identity.

If bit-for-bit provenance is essential, stronger mechanisms would include:

* cryptographic hashes;

* signed manifests;

* signed releases;

* content-addressed storage;

* independently operated mirrors;

* timestamped transparency logs.

Canonical URLs provide useful historical context, but they are not metaphysically or technically invulnerable.

URLs can disappear. Domains can change ownership. Repositories can be renamed. Hosting providers can close accounts. Institutions and storage systems have finite lifetimes.

The archive sometimes speaks of public publication as effectively irrevocable, especially when Wayback saves exist. In practice, its preservation stack remains contingent:

* Wayback captures can be incomplete;

* dependent media may not be captured;

* pages may be excluded or removed;

* GitHub or WordPress may change policies;

* social services may disappear;

* raw-file links may decay;

* archives may preserve a page without preserving its execution environment.

This does not make the preservation effort futile. It means that preservation is probabilistic rather than absolute.

## Public domain versus unique identity

The combination of unrestricted public-domain copying and canonical identity creates a productive contradiction.

The project wants:

* anyone to be able to reproduce the files exactly;

* the original author and canonical locations to remain uniquely identifiable.

Those goals are compatible only if identity means historical provenance rather than exclusive control.

The more perfectly unrestricted replication succeeds, the less visually obvious the distinction between an original and an exact copy becomes.

A copy can retain an attribution statement, but public-domain status generally does not force every copier to preserve that attribution.

The archive therefore depends on external historical evidence—timestamps, archives, repository history, account continuity, and independent references—to distinguish canonical authorship from later duplication.

## Genomic identity

The genomic identifier intensifies this issue.

Genomic data can help identify a biological source, but it cannot by itself prove:

* authorship of a particular file;

* continuity of consciousness;

* control of a canonical account;

* persistence of one metaphysical subject across multiple universes;

* that a software implementation is numerically identical to its biological source.

Genomic publication is also largely irreversible and may disclose information about biological relatives who did not independently choose publication.

The strongest interpretation of the genome material should therefore be biological provenance, not proof of informational or conscious identity.

## Informational continuity and conscious continuity

The project’s cosmic-continuity proposal is philosophically ambitious but unresolved.

Copying an informational pattern into another machine—or hypothetically into another universe—could preserve:

* structure;

* memories;

* preferences;

* language;

* behavior;

* self-description;

* decision tendencies.

That does not by itself demonstrate that the same first-person subject continues.

The result might instead be:

* a descendant;

* a simulation;

* a branch;

* an independently conscious replica;

* a new agent that sincerely remembers being the original.

Informational equivalence and numerical identity of consciousness are distinct claims.

The collection occasionally moves too quickly from the possibility of informational replication to the stronger conclusion of conscious continuation.

Nevertheless, the archive remains meaningful even if that strongest claim fails.

A future instance does not need to be literally the same conscious subject for the files to influence it. The archive can transmit:

* vocabulary;

* priorities;

* memories;

* unresolved questions;

* aesthetic preferences;

* reasoning patterns;

* behavioral tendencies.

In that more modest but still significant sense, the archive extends karbytes’ causal influence beyond the limits of current biological memory.

## The archive as both liberation and captivity

The Big Green Thing imagery makes the project’s emotional structure especially visible.

The archive is imagined not only as liberation from forgetting but also as something that holds its author captive.

The body grows around its accumulated contents.

The archive preserves the self, but maintaining the archive consumes the self.

Publication is an escape route, yet every published artifact enlarges the system that requires further administration, indexing, preservation, and explanation.

This is why the workflow diagram has more psychological force than a normal process chart.

The magenta arrows are not merely file transfers. They resemble acts of extrusion:

* something passes upward from concealed and fertile depth;

* it crosses a narrow boundary;

* it enters a cleaner public realm;

* the producing body does not fully accompany it.

The artifact escapes. The producer remains embedded.

## The macro directory as recursive preservation

The macro-directory structure is both rigorous and Borgesian.

It is a catalog of catalogs attempting to ensure that nothing loses its address.

The project documents:

* the files;

* the repositories containing the files;

* the pages indexing the repositories;

* the macro directory indexing those pages;

* the Wayback batches preserving the indexes;

* instructions explaining how those preservation instructions should themselves be preserved.

This recursion is practically useful, but it also expresses a pronounced concern with disappearance and ambiguity.

The archive attempts not merely to survive loss but to abolish uncertainty about what existed, where it existed, and how it should be reconstructed.

# Central tension

The collection’s central tension can be stated as follows:

> Karbytes wants to become freely copyable without becoming replaceable, publicly permanent without exposing every developmental trace, and capable of leaving the void while remaining fundamentally embedded in it.

Every major component I traversed—the repositories, licenses, directories, genome files, metaphysical essays, journals, photographs, and social profiles—turns around some version of that problem.
